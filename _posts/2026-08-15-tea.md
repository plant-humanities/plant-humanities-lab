# Tea and the Camellia sinensis

"Tea" has come to encapsulate a multitude of meanings: colloquial, cultural and even meanings specific to particular generations of people. At its most basic, tea refers to a beverage prepared by pouring hot water over leaves from the tea plant, the *Camellia sinensis*, an evergreen shrub native to East Asia, the Indian Subcontinent and Southeast Asia. From the tea plant, six major types of tea are produced today: black tea, white tea, oolong tea, green tea, dark tea and pu-er tea, all of which involve different processing methods.

{% include embed/youtube.html vid="lAYRZeDJ4Pc" start="0:4:13" end="0:5:01" %}

There are [two varieties](https://www.kew.org/plants/tea-plant) of *Camellia sinensis*: the *Camellia sinensis var. sinensis* and the *Camellia sinensis var. assamica*, with the latter getting its name from the Assam region of northeastern India where is it widespread. Both varieties are [different](https://youngmountaintea.com/blogs/blog/sinensis-vs-assamica?srsltid=AfmBOopAsEJoRXVYSxncbVLeMiiXHkEyZdQrs8JUjWVd7lYo2b8VFkB-) in appearance: while the *Camellia sinensis var. sinensis* has leaves that are more tender and small, the leaves of the *Camellia sinensis var. assamica* are larger and more robust. In terms of taste, tea produced by processing leaves of the *Sinensis* variety are known to have “flora, fruity and sometimes sweet notes” whereas tea of the *Assam* variety are often characterised as being “stronger, bolder and darker”. Herbal brews are often referred to as tea, but mixes like chamomile, rooibos and peppermint do not derive from the *Camellia sinensis* plant and are tisanes.

# What is “tea”?

The first recorded instance of the word tea in English appeared in 1658 in the *Mercurius Politicus*, an English newspaper. Calling it the “China drink”, Thomas Garraway in advertising the qualities of tea on behalf of his coffee-house wrote about: “That excellent and by all Physicians, approved, China drink, called by the Chineans Tcha, by other nations Tay alias Tee, is sold at the Sultaness Head Coffee-House, in Sweetings Rents, by the Royal Exchange, London.” 

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Garraway%27s_Coffee_House.jpg" %}

In the seventeenth century, Dutch merchants trading in Fujian must have encountered local Fujianese merchants using the dialect term "tay" instead of "cha." It is possible they adopted this term. "Tay" was absorbed into the Dutch lexicon as "thee" which was pronounced exactly the same way as the Fujianese would have pronounced "tay." In turn the English borrowed directly from the Dutch word "thee," giving rise to the word "tea."

# The Practice of Tea Drinking

The practice of tea drinking originates in China and the Chinese attribute the “discovery” of tea consumption to the mythological figure, *Shennong*, the Divine Husbandman (2737–2697 BCE). This painting on the right, by the sixteenth century Ming dynasty painter, Guo Xu (1456–1532), depicts Shennong tasting the leaves of the *Camellia sinensis* plant for its medicinal value. That Shennong was a healer who was interested in plants of medicinal value is corroborated by the father of the Chinese bencao medical tradition, Li Shizhen, in his *Bencao gangmu* (Compendium of Materia Medica) who claims that Shennong tasted hundreds of plants in his search for ones of medicinal value.[^1]

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Guo_Xu_album_dated_1503_(2).jpg" %}

The earliest known physical evidence of tea being consumed was in the second century BC. Archaeologists discovered a mass of partially-decomposed plants, which scientists identified as tea, in the mausoleum of [Emperor Jing of the Han Dynasty](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/archaeologists-find-worlds-oldest-tea-tomb-han-dynasty-emperor-180957790/) in Xian who ruled China over 2000 years ago. This finding indicates that tea from the *Camellia sinensis* was being consumed by Chinese emperors as early as the second century BC.

# Tea and Material Culture: Rituals, Ceremonies, Traditions

As tea consumption spread, so too did rituals and ceremonies associated with tea. From handleless cups with a wide circumference used for both its preparation and consumption, vessels used to drink tea multiplied in shape and form. In Japan, ceramicware was used for the chanoyu: a Japanese ceremonial ritual of tea preparation and consumption, in a specialised space with codified procedures. In Europe, the popularity of Chinese blue and white jingdezhen porcelain led to the creation of Delftware, a type of tin-glazed earthenware, and finally to the success of “hardpaste” porcelain in Meissen, Germany. Today, the revived and much popularised British tradition of afternoon tea with scones, cake, sandwiches and tea (of course!) reminds us of the various ways in which traditions and rituals survive and revive across geography and time, and the transformations that take place in the process.

# Dutch Curiosity: Early European Encounters with Tea

Dutch East India Company (VOC) merchants and affiliates were crucial to the spread of tea to the rest of Europe, particularly Britain. News about tea drinking as well as the commodity itself came to British Isles through Dutch literary sources and directly from their trade with Dutch merchants.[^2] As Markman Ellis remarks, “tea sociability was a conspicuous aspect of Dutch influence on British social life.”[^3] Since 1609, the Japanese granted the VOC trading rights with Japan via the island of Deshima, becoming the only Europeans to successfully establish a trading post in Japan. Through their presence in Japan, they were also able to gain a foothold into the China trade, including the trade in tea. Thus, they became the first Europeans to import tea into Europe from Asia.

On 2 October 1609, the first batch of tea consisting of tea leaves and green powdered tea, set sail to Holland on the *Rood Leeuw met Piljen* ‘red lion with arrows’, via the Dutch controlled port of Bantam, Indonesia.[^4] The Dutch would continue to monopolise the trade in tea until the end of the seventeenth century. The English merchant, William Milburn thus noted that:
>The Dutch East India Company were unquestionably the first who engaged in tea as an article of commerce; and from the beginning until near the close of the seventeenth century, the whole of European demand was supplied through the medium of their sales.[^5]

The English would continue to rely on the Dutch for their supply of tea either from Batavia or from Dutch middlemen in Asia until 1717 when they began shipping tea to England directly from Canton (Guangzhou), China.[^6] Nevertheless, tea was still a luxury item that was imported in small quantities, “one exotic commodity in an array of goods traded by the VOC.”[^7] Accompanying the introduction of tea as a commodity was a curiosity about how to prepare and consume the Chinese beverage. The German naturalist Engelbert Kaempfer (1651-1716), who was chief surgeon of the VOC in Deshima includes a section on the tea plant in his History of Japan (published posthumously in 1727). He also provides illustrations on how tea should be stored and prepared, and tea utensils in which it should be consumed. As Kaempfer was stationed in Japan when he made these observations, they provide insight into Japanese traditions of processing and drinking tea.

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Market_and_trade_in_Bantam_(Banten),_Romeyn_de_Hooghe,_1682-1733.jpg" caption="Romeyn de Hooghe (Printmaker), Market and trade in Bantam (Banten), 1682-1733" %}

Dutch physicians and medical writers were also interested in the medicinal qualities of tea and its effects on human physiology. Apart from Kaempfer, other writers include Nicolaes Tulp (1593–1674), Cornelis Bontekoe (1640–1685) and William Ten Rhijne (1647 – 1700). As Kaempfer’s predecessor, Ten Rhijne wrote the first detailed study of tea, *De Frutice Thee* (On the tea bush), published in 1678 as an appendix to Dutch merchant and naturalist Jacob Breyne’s *Exoticum plantarum centuria prima* (First Century of Exotic Plants). Ten Rhijne’s *De Fructice Thee* includes a long section extolling the “virtues of tea”, where he observed that tea “…purifies the blood, drives away dreadful sleeplessness, wards off malicious vapors from the brain, mitigates vertigo and headaches (especially those arising from a hangover), and is beneficial for dropsy patients, for it is an excellent diuretic.” Knowledge about the commercial and medicinal value of tea spread through these writings; together with information about tea drinking habits, it paved the way for the political and environmental legacies of the nineteenth century.

# Reordering Global Powers

{% include embed/map.html center="0.0000, 0.0000" geojson="tea-map.json" zoom="1" class="full" %}

Tea became a global commodity as trade among world powers increased and products from colonial outposts diversified the market. The French, Dutch, and Portuguese took up tea quickly, but war-torn and impoverished England was slow to adopt the drink in the seventeenth century. This shifted in the eighteenth century when, as England’s economic power expanded and colonial production of other stimulants such as coffee, sugar, and tobacco increased, tea grew in popularity. The thirst for the beverage was matched by the government’s dependence on the revenue, as the Exchequer levied a 100 percent tax on the import.[^8] Demand for tea increased in England as the price fell: it cost £1 for a pound in 1700, when they imported a mere 70,000 pounds of tea from China. By 1750, the British were importing 3,550,000 pounds of tea, and the price per pound was 5s.[^9] Public consumption of tea increased despite the multiple wars and by 1785, England was importing 15 million pounds of tea a year from China.[^10]

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Boston_Tea_Party-Cooper.jpg" caption="On December 16, 1773, a group of men protested tea taxes imposed by England by throwing 342 chests containing tea valued at £18,000 into the water." %}

As small as a cup of tea might be, buying and selling the product was key to a reordering of global power. The American Boston Tea Party of 1773 was emblematic of the economic and imperial power of the commodity. Historians Frank Sanello and Travis Hanes called the colonists  “the greatest tea drinkers in the universe,” but the settlers swallowed their preference for the beverage and dumped forty-six tons of imported Chinese black tea into the harbor.[^11] The protest against British taxation was significant in its defiance of the King in addition to the destruction of such a precious good. While other protests over unfair British rule occurred over the decades, it was this self-denial of a favored beverage that started the American Revolution (1775–1781).

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Opium_War.jpg" caption="French cartoon of an Englishman and his looming troops forcing opium upon the Chinese Emperor, saying ‘I’ll tell you what, you must buy this poison immediately. We want you to poison yourself completely, as we need a lot of tea in order to digest our beefsteaks." %}

Great Britain’s struggle to access Chinese tea over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries reordered global power structures. Spain allied with the United States after the American Revolution and cut off the silver supply to Britain, the only currency the Chinese government accepted. Great Britain imported more products from China than it exported in the eighteenth century, as porcelain, silk, and tea were in high demand in comparison to English wool and iron. Between 1710 and 1759 this trade imbalance drained Britain of silver, as they bought almost three times as much as they sold in China. A nascent illegal opium trade began as early as 1659, when the Dutch smuggled the drug from their outpost in Bengal, India, into China.[^12] The East India Company was opposed to smuggling opium into China at first, as it jeopardized legal trade. They justified their eventual smuggling operation as the only way to acquire the silver for the commodities they wanted to bring back to England. In the same year as the Boston Tea Party, the nascent opium trade earned England’s East India Company (EIC) £39,000 in silver in illegal sales to China. Where the British had failed to entice the Chinese with other products, the EIC smuggled opium worth £250,000 in silver in 1793, which boomed to sales of 7 million Spanish silver dollars between 1806 and 1809.[^13] 


People often ingested opium orally, a less potent form of the poppy product ([Papaver somniferum](https://lab.plant-humanities.org/poppy)), as a sleep aid and relaxant, or at greater doses for a high. Wealthy Chinese users discovered in the early eighteenth century that smoking the rock or combining it with tobacco, increased intensity andproduced a longer high, though it also increased the possibility of addiction.. The debilitating addictive qualities of opium were widely known, and the Chinese economy, government services, and standard of living declined because of its use. An estimated 4 to 12 million people across all social strata in China partook in the pastime before restrictions to its use took effect in 1833.[^14] While wealthy consumers could continue their lives of leisure, opium use was described as an epidemic ruining the lower classes by the Imperial Investigative Censor, Feng Zhanxun: “The poison of opium is that when the people smoke it, they wasted themselves and ruined their businesses; and when the soldiers smoke it, they become tired and break the army’s discipline.”[^15] Military defeats brought shame to the ruling dynasties, as seven out of ten Chinese soldiers used opium: not only were they physically incapable, but they had no desire to fight, which was seen as an abhorrent moral failing.[^16]

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Opium-smokinginChina.(1858).jpg" caption="Article in the *Illustrated London News* that described the process and effects of opium (including poverty and death) in hopes of ending the trade. December 18, 1858." %}

{% include embed/image.html src="China_opium_den.jpg" caption="French pamphlet on opium in China. Anonyme, Société de Saint-Augustin, 1896" %}

The Qing government attempted to reduce opium use with increasingly strict measures to ban its trade. In 1839, they destroyed opium stocks owned by British merchants and the EIC after they failed to comply with the emperor’s edict banning the substance.[^17] Great Britain retaliated to this loss of property with warships, and the two countries fought from 1839 to 1842, the first of the “Opium Wars.” The Treaty of Nanking (Nanjing) was signed in 1842 and is considered the first of the Unequal Treaties between China and Western powers, as China made significant concessions. China surrendered land, was forced to pay reparations for the destroyed opium, and opened more ports to the British.[^18] Tea had become a defining symbol of British identity, but even after the war, China maintained the global monopoly on tea production by safeguarding centuries of trade secrets on how to grow, harvest, and treat the leaves, in addition to stringent measures to keep the plant out of the hands of foreigners. In order to supply tea to the British public, their merchants continued to smuggle opium even after the war, though it had not been made legal to sell it. War broke out again in 1856 when China tried once more to stem the flow of opium into the country.

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Destruction_of_opium_in_1839.jpg" caption="Commissioner Lin and the Destruction of the Opium in 1839." %}

The war ended four brutal years later, with Great Britain gaining even greater economic and cultural dominance. The Chinese government paid more war reparations, opened more ports to European commerce, legalized the opium trade, and granted foreign traders and missionaries rights to travel within the country.[^19] Culturally, Britain asserted dominance by obligating China to use diplomacy in the Western style (no more kowtowing), the beginning of the era in Chinese history known as the Century of Humiliation. The Opium Wars reordered world powers: China was no longer the political powerhouse it had been, defeated by the tiny wet island in Europe with an insatiable thirst for tea.

# Tea's Environmental Legacy

The capability to transplant tea expanded the possibilities for imperial states to invest in their own plantations. Portugal sought part of the tea market, transplanting *Camellia sinensis* in its colonies. Fertile land and an ample supply of labor allowed for the smooth start of Portuguese plantations in South America. With the invasion of Franco-Spanish forces in 1808, the Portuguese royal family fled to Brazil, bringing with them a particular interest in science and botany.[^20] Portuguese curiosity in botany would spur interest in the development of tea plantations, and the beginning of negotiations with the Chinese government to facilitate the journey of tea plant seed to Brazil. Experimental plantations began in the Rio de Janeiro Botanical Garden, with the aid of enslaved African and Chinese laborers.[^21]

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Physical_map_of_Brazil._LOC_2003627071_restored.jpg" %}

Portuguese support for the Brazilian tea industry was crucial, as Dom John VI brought international partnerships and funding for the development of tea plantations. The end of the Napoleonic wars would soon collapse the production of Portuguese tea in Brazil, with Dom John VI returning to Lisbon in 1821 to sooth political discord.[^22] Without a strong leader supporting the industry, efforts to maintain it failed, despite very small successful pushes between 1820 and 1830. A destructive fungus, from the genus *Ceratobasidium* sp. D.P.Rogers, which injures young plants, was the final nail in the coffin. Tea sprouts and young leaves were killed before they had a chance to flourish, making it impossible to maintain already struggling plantations. Ultimately, this crisis prevented the success of cultivating tea commercially in Brazil for years to come.

However, the story of the *Camellia sinensis* in Brazil does not end with the nineteenth century. Even though many plantations from Brazil were abandoned in the 1800s, as a result of these plantations, today the plant grows in the Atlantic Forest, an extensive forest in Brazil, where it has begun outcompeting native species of plant families like *Rubiaceae*, *Piperaceae*, and *Melastomataceae*, all crucial parts of the Brazilian ecosystem. While the tea plant has not been labeled as invasive, studies are revealing its capacity for subduing native plants;[^23] the plant is creating a hostile environment for endangered and native species that are already at risk.

# The Georgian Tea Upshot

Across the world, in the 1830s, Prince Miha Eristavi of Georgia, in the Caucasus region, traveled to Chinato to smuggle home tea seeds of the *Camellia sinensis* plant. Impressed by the taste of the plant, he harvested it himself and used his seeds to begin the first tea plantations in Georgia.[^24] While these early tea plantations were largely unsuccessful, with the help of Russia's imperial strength and plantation support, the Georgian tea industry enjoyed massive growth by the 1860s.

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:tea_plantations_in_Chakva.jpg" caption="Georgian tea plantation between 1905 and 1915." %}
{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Physical_Map_of_Georgia_(Stripped)_(en).svg" caption="Map of Georgia." %}

Georgia became a tea-exporting powerhouse during the nineteenth century and later became a central asset of the Soviet Union. The Georgian plantations we see today have a complex relationship with the environments out of which they were carved. They face battles that are not uncommon in tea plantations globally; issues of sustainability and the welfare of the *Camellia sinensis* are persistent. 

{% include embed/youtube.html vid="KN9z94KPaV4" end="2:05" caption="This is a video showing current Georgian tea-planters attempting to revive tea production to Soviet scales." %}
{% include embed/image.html src="wc:1951_CPA_1603.jpg" caption="Soviet postage stamp showcasing Georgian tea harvest, 1951." %}

The nineteenth century also saw an important shift toward monoculture plantations, which would have a long-lasting negative impact on local flora and fauna.[^25] Land-use changes molded the Georgian environmental landscape to fit the monoculture model, making natural ecosystems more vulnerable to disease, pests, and erosion. Despite the decline in tea production at the end of the twentieth century, the nineteenth-century legacies of Georgian plantations linger.

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Prokudin-Gorsky._Tea_plantations._Chakva.jpg" %}

The present-day introduction of chemical and mineral inputs poses serious concerns to the Georgian tea industry.[^26] Its use of pesticides and herbicides to combat the spread of weeds and pests is a threat to clean water sources, biodiversity, and human/animal health. Fertilizer usage, especially mineral-based manuring, is a high environmental risk for the soil, water, and humans that surround a plantation. Outside of chemical interventions, irrigational damage on plantations pose high environmental hazards. The water source and the design of these systems can perpetuate the degradation of soil and unsustainable water usage.

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:08_-_tea_collection_near_Achigvara.jpg" %}

With these issues in mind, many Georgian plantations have begun integrating more sustainable practices. Many plantations have begun organic farming, refusing to use chemical fertilizers and pesticides that present risks to local ecosystems.[^27] Artisanal tea planting, where tea is produced in smaller batches on smaller pieces of land, is a recent trend aimed at decreasing Georgian reliance on massive monoculture farms. The United Nations Development Program  and the European Union have provided aid to Georgian farmers, particularly smaller tea producers, to help them transition to environmentally conscious production practices and packaging. While these initiatives do not address all the issues presented by plantations, producers and governmental agencies are more optimistic about the future of sustainably produced and managed Georgian tea plantations. These plantations will continue to grapple with questions of sustainability and attempt to integrate better alternatives to these pressing problems.

# The Mutability of Meaning

Meanings associated with tea have shifted across time and space, changing with the people and cultures that live with it. The case studies from the nineteenth century give a snapshot of what tea meant to different people in different regions; the plant itself was a symbol for revolution, the impetus for a series of bloody wars, and the center of global economic shifts. The multiplicity of these associations demonstrates that the tea plant cannot be viewed in isolation, but it must be placed within the context of the environments in which it grows.

{% include embed/map.html geojson="tea-map.json" center="18, 0" zoom="1" caption="This is a map showcasing the different locations discussed in this article. Made by the authors." %}

Despite all the disparate transformations the plant has undergone, the *Camellia sinensis* species remains ubiquitous among many different cultures globally. Historically, the plant changed the fate of wars and empires, and today it moves more subtly through daily, often mundane, routines. Many traditions, rituals, and ceremonies continue to celebrate tea, carrying the historical weight of the nineteenth century. Despite this, the plant has remained resilient. Even through centuries of war and environmental destruction, *Camellia sinensis* lives on in plantations, gardens, laboratories, and homes, and remains a potent piece of human life and history. Every pot steeped, every kettle poured, and every cup sipped is evidence of the enduring legacy of this influential plant.

{% include embed/image-compare.html before="Camellia Sinensus_IMG_1.jpeg" after="Camellia Sinensus_IMG_2.jpg" %}

Camellia sinensis has certainly evolved from its early beginnings in Europe as a luxury drink. Today, tea can refer to matcha, sweet tea, bubble tea, masala chai, or the various tea bags stored in kitchen cabinets. Many cultures use tea in foods and beverages, from drinks such as Earl Grey lattes to dishes like Chinese tea-smoked duck and the Japanese Ochazuke (where green tea is poured over rice), or even green tea ice cream from grocery stores. Perhaps, then, it is the taste of this plant and its deep ties to human cultures that continue to draw our attention. Regardless of whether it is raspberry green tea or the crates of tea that launched the Boston Tea Party, the tea plant has remained the same. The only difference is the multilayered meanings now attached to it and that continue to live on: culturally, economically, and politically.
